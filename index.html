    About This Project

    Coral reefs are facing unprecedented environmental threats. This project will investigate the microbiome of Orbicella faveolata, an endangered Caribbean reef-building coral, in Puerto Morelos, Mexico. Through field sampling and laboratory based microbial analysis, we aim to characterize its associated microbial communities and relate them to observed coral health. We hypothesize that the microbial profiles of healthy versus degraded coral colonies will reveal key insights about coral resiliency.

    Join The Discussion

    What is the context of this research?

    Coral reefs support nearly 25% of marine species while providing food and livelihoods for millions of people (Bhuyan et al., 2026). By reducing wave energy and coastal erosion, healthy reefs also protect communities from climate related impacts. However, rising ocean temperatures and acidification increasingly threaten these ecosystems, with over 44% of reef-building corals facing an elevated risk of extinction (ICUN, 2024). Corals depend on complex microbial communities that influence their health and responses to environmental stress (Voolstra et al., 2024). Building upon prior research characterizing coral microbiomes across growth stages (Perez-Trejo et al., 2025), this project will characterize microbial communities associated with colonies O. faveolata of varying health conditions. I hypothesize that differences in microbial composition may be associated with variation in observed coral health, and contribute to our understanding of coral resilience.

    What is the significance of this project?

    Understanding the microorganisms associated with reef-building corals may provide important insight into coral health, disease, and resilience. Although research has established that microbial communities are closely related to coral biology, important gaps remain in understanding these relationships across Caribbean coral populations. Recent research has demonstrated differences in the bacterial communities associated with Orbicella faveolata across life-cycle stages and growth conditions (Perez-Trejo et al., 2025). By characterizing the coral microbiome of both healthy and degraded colonies of Orbicella faveolata in Puerto Morelos, this project will contribute valuable baseline information about its associated microbial communities. These findings can support future research investigating microbial species that may positively impact coral health in the face of rising environmental stressors and inform future conservation strategies in the Mexican Caribbean reef system.

    What are the goals of the project?

    Beginning in January 2027, I plan to conduct fieldwork in Puerto Morelos, Mexico, in collaboration with researchers at UNAM. After assessing reef condition and recording observations, we will collect coral samples from both healthy and degraded Orbicella faveolata colonies and prepare them for laboratory analysis. Utilizing 16S rRNA sequencing we aim to characterize the bacterial communities associated with the sampled corals and examine patterns in their microbial composition. Following sample processing and sequencing, we will analyze the resulting data to identify the microbial communities present and interpret the findings in the context of environmental observations of coral health. The overall goal of this project is to determine microbial species that may play a role in coral resilience and establish data within healthy and degraded Orbicella faveolata colonies.

    Additional Information

    Between 1985 and 2025, ocean waters in coral reef regions rose by 0.81 degrees Celsius and in that same time frame coral cover decreased by 9.5% (GCRMN, 2025). Climate change is undeniably and alarmingly impacting an ecosystem we must depend on to mitigate additional effects on our warming world, such as erosion and coastal flooding. Reef-building corals refer to corals that actively contribute to reef architecture and growth -- by focusing on Orbicella faveolata, this study will generate additional information on the microbial communities associated with an important reef-building species. Establishing this baseline may help guide future investigations into how environmental conditions influence coral-associated microorganisms and their relationships with coral health. Ultimately, I hope this work will contribute to a broader understanding of reef ecosystem functioning and inform future coral monitoring and conservation efforts.

    This project will be conducted in collaboration with researchers at the Universidad Nacional Autonoma de Mexico, providing an opportunity to contribute to ongoing coral research in the Mexican Caribbean while benefitting from established local scientific expertise. This collaboration will also ensure that fieldwork is conducted responsibly and in accordance with Mexican research regulations, environmental protections, and permitting requirements. Observed coral condition will be evaluated through visual assessments of O. faveolata according to indications of compromised health. All coral assessment, collection, sampling, and laboratory activities and analysis will follow appropriate scientific and ethical practices and be conducted by certified SCUBA divers.
